The Situation

A 4-year-old Series-B SaaS company in Bengaluru sells subscription HR & Payroll software to Indian small and mid-sized businesses. It has three plan tiers — Starter, Growth, Enterprise.

At year-end, the picture looked like this:

  • ▸ Revenue: ₹73.2 Cr actual vs ₹78 Cr planned — 94% achievement
  • ▸ EBITDA: ₹1.2 Cr actual vs ₹4.6 Cr planned — only 26% of planned profit
  • ▸ Sales head said: “Demand environment is tough.”
  • ▸ CRO (Chief Revenue Officer) said: “Pricing pressure from competitors.”
  • ▸ CTO said: “Engineering overspend was board-approved.”
The Brief from the CFO

“Board is meeting in 2 weeks. Everyone has a different story. Build me a clean variance bridge from ₹4.6 Cr planned EBITDA to ₹1.2 Cr actual. Tell me who owns each rupee and what we change for FY25.”

How I Approached It

A 5-step variance forensic — comparing apples to apples.

Build a Flexible Budget

The original plan assumed a certain sales volume. Actual volume was different — so I re-flexed the budget at actual volume. This way, when comparing budget vs actual, we’re not mixing up “you sold less” with “you spent more per unit”.

Three-Way Variance

For revenue, I split the gap into three parts: Volume (how many we sold), Price (how much we charged), and Mix (which plans they bought). Same approach for variable costs.

Cost Variance by Function

For each cost bucket — Sales & Marketing, R&D, Customer Success, G&A — I split the variance into rate, efficiency, and volume. Each gets a clear owner.

Find the Root Cause

Numbers tell you what happened. They don’t tell you why. I traced each major variance back to a real-world reason — by reading CRM extracts, talking to function heads, and checking the commission plan.

Recommend Process Changes

The output is not just a report. It’s a changed FY25 budget process — with mix-locked targets, gross-margin-based commissions, and monthly variance reviews.

The 6 Key Insights

1

The miss is mix, not volume

Volume was only ₹0.31 Cr off. Mix shift was ₹2.10 Cr — 7× larger. Yet leadership was treating it as a sales-volume problem.

₹2.10 Cr
2

The commission plan caused the mix shift

Reps earned the same % on Starter and Enterprise. Starter is easier to sell. Naturally, the funnel skewed cheap. Owner: CRO.

Process
3

Pricing pressure was real but small

Price variance was only ₹0.18 Cr — about 5% of the gap. The “competitive pricing” narrative was overstated.

₹0.18 Cr
4

Marketing went over plan without ROI proof

S&M overspend of ₹0.55 Cr came mainly from outbound campaigns with no tracked CAC payback. Money spent, results unclear.

₹0.55 Cr
5

R&D scope creep on 2 features

Two features doubled in original spec scope mid-build. Engineering delivered, but cost ₹0.32 Cr more than budget. Needs gating process.

₹0.32 Cr
6

SaaS unit economics confirm it

NRR (Net Revenue Retention) held at 109%, but new-customer LTV/CAC dropped from 3.4× to 1.9× — exactly what mix-shift predicts.

Confirmed

What FY25 Looks Like — 5 Process Changes

The output is not just a report. It’s a changed budget and incentive process that prevents the same problem next year.

# Change What It Fixes Owner By When
1 Tie sales commission to gross-margin contribution, not gross revenue Removes the incentive to chase low-margin Starter deals CRO + Finance Q1 FY25
2 Plan FY25 budget with mix-locked targets Forces leadership to declare expected mix upfront — no hiding Finance Pre-Q1 FY25
3 Monthly variance review with mix as a separate line Catches mix drift in 30 days, not 365 Finance + CRO From Apr-FY25
4 Marketing spend gated by CAC payback < 12 months Stops campaigns without a profitability test CMO + Finance Q1 FY25
5 R&D scope-change board (any >15% increase) Prevents scope creep from silently overrunning budget CTO + CFO Q1 FY25

Why This Project Matters

Most variance reports stop at “revenue down, costs up”. This one names the mechanism, names the owner, and rewires the process.

  • ▸ Assigns blame correctly. The mix-shift insight rescues sales from being mis-blamed and points at the commission design.
  • ▸ Produces a process change, not a one-time report. FY25 will use mix-locked targets and gross-margin-based commissions.
  • ▸ Bridges FP&A and CMA. Uses both classical variance analysis and SaaS unit economics (NRR, LTV/CAC) — most candidates know one or the other, not both.
  • ▸ Defendable in a boardroom. Every rupee in the bridge has a name attached.
